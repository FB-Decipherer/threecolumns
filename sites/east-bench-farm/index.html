What this is

A family farm on irrigated ground east of Worland, watered by a center pivot with the Bighorns in view. Alfalfa hay by the bale or the ton, certified weed-free for the mountains, and beef by the quarter, half or whole. Our sugar beets and malt barley go under contract to buyers in Worland.

A  Plain answers
What do you sell?

Alfalfa hay, and beef by the share. That is all we sell to the public.

  • Hay — alfalfa, in small squares you can lift by hand, and in 3x4 large squares by the ton. Certified weed-free when you ask for it.
  • Beef — a quarter, a half or a whole steer, once a year in the fall. You buy a share of the live animal, and the processor cuts it to your order.

Our sugar beets and malt barley are grown under contract and go to buyers in Worland910. They are not for sale here.

What does hay cost?

By the ton for large squares, by the bale for small ones. This season’s price for good alfalfa in 3x4 large squares is $285 a ton at the stack — a sample figure for this demonstration, set inside the range in the state’s own hay report: $270 to $300 a ton on 3 September 20262.

Small squares are priced by the bale. Delivery is extra; ask when you call.

Is your hay certified weed-free? Why does it matter?

Yes, when you ask for it. On the Bighorn National Forest, and every national forest in the region, you may not bring hay, straw or grain onto the forest unless it is certified weed-free and every bale carries the tag or twine that proves it3. Hunters, outfitters and anyone packing horses into the mountains need it.

Certified means the county Weed and Pest district inspected the field4. The paperwork comes with the hay; keep it with the bales.

How does buying a quarter of beef work?
  1. You reserve a quarter, a half or a whole steer before it goes to the processor. You are buying a share of the live animal7.
  2. We haul it to a custom-exempt processor in the Basin, and you tell the processor how you want it cut and wrapped.
  3. You pay us by the pound of hanging weight — the carcass as it hangs after slaughter — and you pay the processor for the cutting and wrapping.
  4. You pick up your beef at the processor, frozen and wrapped. The packages are stamped “Not for Sale”: they are for your household6.

You bring home less than the hanging weight, because of bone and trim. The processor can tell you what to expect from the way you ask for it to be cut.

Why can’t I just buy a steak from you?

Because Wyoming law does not allow it from a farm like ours. Meat from a custom-exempt plant may not be sold to stores or to anyone else; packaged cuts for sale have to come from an inspected plant5. What the law does allow is selling a share of the animal before it is butchered7, which is why we do it that way.

Where are you, and when can I come?

East of Worland, on the bench above the river, by appointment. Call or text first and we will send directions; we are often in the field.

Call or text (307) 555-0142. (A made-up number, from the 555-0100 to 555-0199 range set aside for examples like this one.)

Do you deliver?

Hay, yes: large squares by the truckload, anywhere in Washakie County. Small squares are loaded at the farm. Beef is picked up at the processor.

Why a website, and not just Facebook?

We are on Facebook too. This page is the one that is always here and always right: no login to read it, nothing to scroll past, this season’s price and what is available in one place. And it belongs to the farm, the way the pivot does. Column C has the detail.

If you have a Facebook account, you may as well keep it, and add a link to your own Three Columns site as well.

Who are you?

A family farm: two generations on the same irrigated ground east of Worland. Hay, beef, beets and barley, one pivot, and a good many years of ditch work before it.

C  How it works (Details)

The farm in detail: the ground, the water, the crops, and the rules behind every answer in column A.

The ground and the water

The farm sits at about 4,000 feet, in the driest part of Wyoming: the lower Big Horn Basin gets 7 to 8 inches of rain and snow a year13. Nothing here grows on rainfall alone. The water comes from the Big Horn River by canal, and Washakie County irrigates 38,025 acres that way1.

The pivot

The alfalfa is watered by a center pivot: a line of pipe on wheeled towers, turning slowly around a fixed point at the middle of the field where the water comes in, and dropping it close to the crop. Before the pivot, the field was flood-irrigated from the ditch.

The difference is large. In the Highland Hanover Irrigation District, fields changed from flood irrigation to pivots went from under 50 percent efficient to over 85 percent11, paid for in part through the Natural Resources Conservation Service’s EQIP program, which counts sprinkler systems in the Hanover area among its priorities12.

A pivot waters a circle, so the corners of a square field stay dry — the tan corners in the picture at the top of the page.

The rotation

Alfalfa stays in for several years; then the ground goes to sugar beets and malt barley before it returns to hay. Those are the county’s leading crops by acreage: hay and forage on 20,022 acres, barley on 6,329, sugar beets on 5,2981.

The beets go to the factory in Worland, bought by the community in 2002 and a grower-owned cooperative since 20189. The barley is grown under contract for malting and goes to the elevator a few miles outside town10. Neither is sold here.

The hay, in detail

Three cuttings in a good year, and each is tested for feed value; ask and we will send the report. Certified weed-free hay comes from fields the county Weed and Pest district has inspected; those bales carry the tags or twine, and come with the certificate, that the Forest Service asks to see34.

The price follows the market. The USDA’s Wyoming Direct Hay Report comes out every week or two from Torrington2, and the price in column A sits inside its range for the same kind and grade of hay.

The beef, in detail

A share has to be spoken for before slaughter, and that is what makes it legal. Wyoming lets a farm sell a live animal, or a portion of one, before slaughter for future delivery7, and one steer can go to four customers as quarters, with proper records6. What comes back is custom-exempt meat, marked “Not for Sale”: for the owner’s household, never for resale5.

The steer is brand-inspected, as the Livestock Board requires whenever livestock change owners or cross a county line8. A 2024 change to the Food Freedom Act would let farms sell uninspected beef directly, but it takes effect only when the governor certifies that federal law allows it7.

Who keeps this page

The farm does not build web pages, and does not have to. Behind this demonstration, we are hoping the county’s chapters of the Future Farmers of America — Chief Washakie FFA at Worland High School, and Ten Sleep FFA, both rated Gold in 202614 — will want to act as ambassadors: bringing pages like this one to the farms, and each farm’s news to its page. Whether they will be interested is not yet known. If they are, when the third cutting is in, the farm sends a text, and the card at the top of the middle column changes.

The pilot program that would pay for the first dozen pages like this one is proposed at washakie-ai.online15.
