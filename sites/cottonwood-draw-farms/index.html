What this is

We hire a seasonal crew for the beet and barley harvest, east of Worland. This page says what the work is, what the law says you are owed, and who to call if something is wrong. It is written to be read by the people doing the work.

A  Plain answers
What is the work, and when?

Beet and barley harvest, and the hay that comes before it. Late September into November, weather deciding the exact days.

  • Sugar beets — running with the harvester and the trucks, piling, and cleaning up the headlands.
  • Malt barley — grain cart, truck driving, and the elevator run.
  • Hay — stacking and hauling, earlier in the season.

Sugar beets and barley are two of the three largest crops in this county by acreage21. The season is short and the days are long while it lasts.

How much does the job pay?

This page does not print a wage figure, on purpose. Not because we are hiding it — the rate is in the job order you are given, and we will tell you on the telephone. Because the legal floor under that rate changes, and a number typed into a web page goes stale and then misleads the person who most needs it to be right.

What the law actually requires is that you be offered and paid the highest of several figures: the adverse effect wage rate, an approved prevailing wage, any collective bargaining rate, the federal minimum wage, the state minimum wage, or whatever higher rate the employer intends to pay2.

For a job filled through the H‑2A program, the one that usually governs is the adverse effect wage rate, or AEWR. The current rates are published by the Department of Labor, by occupation code and by state, in one place3. That is where to look on the day you need it.

And one thing you should know before you look. On 26 August 2026 a federal court held that the method the Department of Labor was using to set those rates was unlawful, and ordered the Department to produce a new one. The rates already published still apply for now, and the Department has said employers should keep paying them. But it has also said that workers employed between then and whenever the new method arrives may later be owed a wage adjustment for that period4. If you work this harvest, keep your pay stubs.

One protection does not move: if a new rate published during your contract is lower than the rate you were guaranteed, the employer must still pay the rate you were guaranteed2. Your pay cannot be cut in the middle of the season because a formula in Washington changed.

Does Wyoming’s minimum wage apply to me?

No. Wyoming’s minimum wage law defines who counts as an employee, and the definition says it “shall not include … any individual employed in agriculture”5. Farm work is excluded outright.

So the state figure is not your floor, and anyone who quotes it to you — in either direction — is quoting the wrong law. Your floor is the federal minimum wage where federal wage law reaches the farm, or the AEWR and prevailing wage rules above if the job came through H‑2A.

Am I guaranteed any hours?

If you are here on an H‑2A visa, or doing the same work alongside that crew, yes. The employer has to offer you work for at least three‑fourths of the workdays in your contract period. If less work is offered than that, the employer owes you what you would have earned had you worked the guaranteed days1.

The regulation does the arithmetic itself: a ten‑week contract at six days a week and eight hours a day is 480 hours, so the guarantee is 360 hours1. Your own Sabbath and the federal holidays do not count against it.

This is why your pay stub has to show guaranteed hours separately from hours above the guarantee — so you can check it yourself1.

Is the housing free? What condition does it have to be in?

At no cost to H‑2A workers, and to anyone doing the same work who cannot reasonably get home at the end of the day1. Not deducted, not a deposit, not rent.

It also has to meet federal housing standards — a real list, covering water, toilets, cooking, beds, heating, screens and so on. Which list applies turns on an odd detail: housing built from 3 April 1980 onward must meet the Occupational Safety and Health Administration’s standards, while older housing may meet either those or an earlier set13.

You are also owed either three meals a day or free and convenient cooking facilities to make your own. If meals are provided and charged for, the charge has a legal maximum and must be stated in the job offer1.

Who pays to get me here, and to the field each day?
  1. Getting here. Once you complete half of the contract period, the employer must pay your reasonable travel and daily subsistence cost from where you came from — in this country or abroad — if it was not advanced to you already1.
  2. To the field. Transportation between employer housing and the worksite is at no cost to you1.
  3. Going home. At the end of the contract, or if you are let go without cause and have no other H‑2A job to go to, the employer provides or pays for the trip back1.

The vehicle matters too. A crew vehicle must carry insurance of not less than $100,000 for each seat12, and must meet safety standards on lights, brakes, tires, steering and loading, with a seat for every person riding12.

Someone asked me for money to get this job. Is that allowed?

No. The rule is flat: the employer and its agents must not have “sought or received payment of any kind” from a worker for anything connected to getting the job certified7.

It reaches the recruiter as well. The employer must put it in writing in its contract with any foreign labor contractor or recruiter that they may not seek or take money from workers either7.

If you paid someone to get this job, that is worth a telephone call to the number at the bottom of this column. Keep whatever receipt, message or note you have.

Should I have something in writing?

Yes, and in a language you understand1. The timing is set:

  • An H‑2A worker gets the work contract no later than when applying for the visa.
  • A worker doing the same work who is already here gets it no later than the first day of work.

If no separate contract is handed to you, then the job order is your contract, together with whatever the law requires on top of it1. So keep the job order. It is the document.

Water, a toilet, and somewhere to wash — in the field?

Where eleven or more people are doing hand labor in the field on a given day, the employer must provide drinking water, a toilet and handwashing facilities8. The water has to be drinkable, “suitably cool,” in enough quantity for the heat and the work, and placed where everyone can actually reach it8.

Here is the part almost everyone gets wrong in Wyoming. For most workplace safety questions in this state you call Wyoming’s own safety agency. But field sanitation is one of the things Wyoming did not take on. Drinking water, toilets and handwashing in the field, and farm labor camps, are enforced here by the federal Wage and Hour Division instead9. Calling the wrong one costs you weeks.
What happens if I get hurt?

This is the most important answer on the page, because Wyoming is unusual and the answer depends on how you were hired.

Wyoming requires workers’ compensation coverage only for employments it lists as extra‑hazardous. In the agriculture sector, the only listed entry is logging. Crop production and animal production are not on the list at all, which means coverage for ordinary farm and ranch work is optional — something an employer may elect to carry, not something the state requires6.

But if you are here through H‑2A, you must be covered anyway. Federal law anticipates exactly this situation: where a state exempts agricultural work from its compensation system, the H‑2A employer has to provide insurance giving benefits at least equal to what the state’s compensation law would give for comparable work, at no cost to you — and must file proof of it with the government before being certified to hire at all1.

So two people can be stacking the same hay with different protection. The guest worker is covered by federal requirement. The neighbor hired locally may have no coverage unless this farm chose to carry it. Ask before you start, and ask for the answer in writing. It is a fair question and a careful employer will not mind it.
What should my pay stub show?

On or before each payday, in writing1:

  • Total earnings for the pay period, and the hourly or piece rate.
  • Hours offered, shown separately as three‑fourths‑guarantee hours and hours above the guarantee.
  • Hours actually worked.
  • Every deduction, itemized.
  • Units produced each day, if you are paid by the piece.
  • The pay period dates, and the employer’s name, address and federal identification number.

Pay comes at least twice a month, or more often if that is the local practice1. A deduction is not lawful if it carries a profit to the employer, or is mainly for the employer’s own benefit or convenience1.

If you are not on an H‑2A visa, a parallel set of rules reaches the same place: itemized written statement each pay period, and payroll records kept for three years11.

Is the visa crew covered by the same law I am?

No, and this surprises people. Two crews can work the same field under two different statutes.

  • Workers here on an H‑2A visa are covered by the H‑2A rules — the job-offer requirements this column keeps citing1.
  • Migrant and seasonal workers who are not on that visa are covered by a different law, the Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act, which requires written disclosure of the terms when you are recruited, wage statements, vehicle safety and insurance, and housing standards11.

The second law expressly excludes H‑2A workers from its definitions10. They are not left unprotected — they are protected by the other set. But it does mean the answer to “what am I owed” starts with how you were hired.

That law also gives you two things you control yourself: a retaliation complaint to the Secretary of Labor within 180 days, and the right to sue in federal court without going through any agency first14.

Something is wrong. Who do I call?

Wage and Hour Division, U.S. Department of Labor — 1‑866‑487‑9243 (1‑866‑4‑US‑WAGE), 8am to 5pm in your own time zone. This is the right number for wages, the hour guarantee, housing, transportation, recruitment fees, and — in Wyoming — field sanitation18.

Wyoming has no Wage and Hour office of its own; the district office covering this state is in Salt Lake City20.

What the Department says about calling:

  • Your name, the nature of the complaint, and whether a complaint exists at all may not be disclosed15.
  • It “does not ask about the immigration status of employees,” except as needed to enforce the temporary labor certification programs themselves, and does not share information that could identify you to another agency15.
  • Retaliating against you for complaining or cooperating is itself unlawful15.

A second door, much less known. You can walk into a Wyoming workforce center and file a complaint there about a violation of employment law. The state has to take it and refer it on, and because you are a farmworker the clock runs faster than it does for other complaints17. The state’s farmworker program office is in Riverton19.

For safety matters other than field sanitation: Wyoming OSHA, (307) 777‑77869.

Is all of this actually being enforced right now?

An honest answer: most of it, but not all, and we would rather tell you which.

A set of newer H‑2A protections added in 2024 is still printed in the regulations but is not currently being enforced. The Department of Labor instructed its own investigators to suspend enforcement of everything the 2024 rule added, after courts in several states blocked parts of it, and a proposal to remove those provisions is pending and not final16.

Everything this column tells you — the hour guarantee, no-cost housing to federal standards, meals or a kitchen, the three transportation duties, no recruitment fees, the written contract, workers’ compensation, the pay stub, the limits on deductions — comes from the older rules and is not affected by that suspension16.

What is affected is a thinner layer on top: seat belt requirements in crew vehicles, progressive discipline before a for-cause termination, and some disclosure items. Those may or may not survive. We have left them out of this column rather than tell you that you have something you may not be able to enforce.

Why a web page, and not a flyer at the store?

We put up flyers too. This page is the one that is always here, always the same, and always in both languages: nothing to log into, nothing to scroll past, the work and the law in one place.

And it is honest about its own limits. Where the law is unsettled, it says so. Where a number would go stale, it sends you to the place the number actually lives. Column C has the detail, and the middle column shows you every source before you decide whether to go look at it yourself.

20 CFR 655.120 — Offered wage rate
Employment and Training Administration, U.S. Department of Labor · amended 88 FR 12801, 89 FR 34060, 90 FR 47963 (2 October 2025)

10Statute · definitions, and an exclusion that matters
29 U.S.C. 1802 — Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act: definitions
United States Code · Cornell Legal Information Institute
Defines a migrant agricultural worker as one employed in seasonal or temporary agricultural employment who must be absent overnight from their permanent residence, and a seasonal agricultural worker as one in the same employment who need not be. 14Statute · two remedies the worker controls

Requires every state workforce agency to accept complaints alleging violations of employment-related law and to refer them to the enforcement agency, and to notify the complainant which agency received it. 22Rulemaking · a rule that went away in September
Rescission of Coordinated Enforcement Regulations
Final rule · Employment and Training Administration, Wage and Hour Division and OSHA, U.S. Department of Labor · 91 FR 55744, published 31 August 2026, effective 30 September 2026
Removes and reserves 29 CFR part 42, which had set formal procedures for coordinating migrant and seasonal farmworker enforcement among the Wage and Hour Division, OSHA and the Employment and Training Administration. The Department’s stated reason is that the procedures were obsolete, no longer reflected its structure or practices, and had not been consistently implemented for decades, and that the substantive protections remain in the farmworker statute and the H‑2A regulations.
Why it is cited: it took effect two days before this page was written, and it is the reason the page describes who enforces what by naming the agencies directly rather than citing the coordination rule a reader would now find empty.

Prior know-how is assumed here, and the items become more “technical” toward the bottom. How the wage floor is actually computed, which law reaches which worker, what the courts have just done to all of it, and why this page prints no rate.

Two crews, two statutes

A Washakie County farm at harvest can have two kinds of worker in the same field, and they are protected by different bodies of law that barely touch.

Guest workers on an H‑2A visa are covered by the job-offer regulation at 20 CFR 655.122 and enforced under 29 CFR part 5011. That is where the hour guarantee, no-cost housing, the transportation duties, the meal obligation and the workers’ compensation override all live.

Migrant and seasonal workers not on that visa are covered by the Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act, 29 U.S.C. 1801 and following, with regulations at 29 CFR part 50011. That is where recruitment disclosure, wage statements, vehicle insurance and housing certification live.

The division is deliberate and explicit: the farmworker act excludes H‑2A workers from both of its worker definitions10. So the first question in any dispute is not what happened but how the worker was hired, because that determines which statute to open.

There is a second split inside the farmworker act worth knowing. Its small-business exemption borrows the federal wage law’s man-days test, so a farm below that threshold sits outside the act altogether — except as to any farm labor contractor it hires, who remains covered10.

How the wage floor is computed, and why no number is printed here

The adverse effect wage rate exists so that bringing in foreign agricultural labor does not pull down the wages of workers already here. It is not the wage; it is one of six floors, and the employer owes the highest of them2.

Until recently there was essentially one rate per state. Since 2023 the rates are keyed to occupational classification codes, and since the rule published 2 October 2025 they are computed from Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational wage data at two skill levels — an entry level set from the first third of the wage distribution, and an experienced level set from the whole of it — with a downward adjustment where the employer houses the worker, capped at 30 percent of the rate2. The relevant occupation is the one whose duties the worker performs on more than half the workdays of the contract2.

That change is the practical reason this page carries no figure. There is no longer “the Wyoming rate” to quote. There is a rate for an occupation code at a skill level in a state, published in a spreadsheet that is replaced when rates are reissued3. Any number typed into this page would be wrong for most readers immediately and wrong for all of them eventually.

The one piece of that machinery a worker should carry in their head is the ratchet at 20 CFR 655.120(b)(6): where a newly published rate is lower than the rate guaranteed in the job order, the employer must go on paying the guaranteed rate2. Rates revised mid-season can raise a wage and cannot lower a guarantee.

What the court did on 26 August 2026

A federal court in the Eastern District of California held the Department’s wage-setting method unlawful and ordered it to produce a new one and publish new rates promptly. It did not vacate the rule, so the published rates continue to apply4.

The Department’s own announcement says two things that sit awkwardly together, and both are worth quoting rather than smoothing over. Employers “should continue to adhere to all applicable laws” and current rates “remain applicable at this time.” And employers whose workers were paid under the challenged method during what the Department calls the backpay period “may be required to make wage adjustment payments” — while “no such payment obligation exists at this time”4.

Translated for a person stacking beets: your rate stands, and there is a live possibility that a later adjustment is owed for this season. No deadline for the new method has been announced. The only sensible act available to a worker is to keep every pay stub and the job order, which is why column A says so plainly.

On the books and not enforced: an honest map

A 2024 rule added a layer of H‑2A protections. Courts in Georgia and Kentucky enjoined parts of it for groups of states and associations, a court in Mississippi stayed two subsections nationwide, and a court in North Carolina upheld the rule outright. In June 2025 the Wage and Hour Division instructed its own investigators to suspend enforcement of every provision the 2024 rule added, and a rescission was proposed in July 2025 that had not been finalized as of 2 October 202616.

This produces a genuinely strange state of affairs: a reader can open the current regulation, find a protection printed in it, and be unable to get it enforced. Anyone writing a worker-facing page has to decide what to do about that. This page states the older protections as live, because they are, and leaves the 2024 additions out of column A rather than describing an entitlement a worker cannot act on.

For the record, the suspended layer includes seat belt requirements in employer transportation, the progressive-discipline conditions before a termination for cause, piece-rate and recruiter disclosure items, immediate effectiveness of a new wage rate, and guest access to employer housing16.

Wyoming’s two gaps

Two features of Wyoming law shape this page more than anything federal.

The minimum wage does not reach farm work at all. The state statute’s definition of employee excludes “any individual employed in agriculture”5. The exclusion is categorical, not a lower rate.

Workers’ compensation is elective for farm work. Wyoming requires coverage only for employments on its extrahazardous list, and the only agricultural entry on that list is logging — crop production and animal production are absent6. An employer may elect coverage for all employees and may withdraw after two years6.

The interaction with federal law is the striking part. Because Wyoming exempts agriculture, an H‑2A employer is required by 20 CFR 655.122(e) to provide insurance giving benefits at least equal to what the state system would give for comparable work, at no cost to the worker, with proof filed before certification1. The result is that the federal program closes a hole in state law for guest workers and leaves it open for locals. On a farm that has not elected coverage, the visa holder is insured and the neighbor is not.

Who enforces what, in this state

Wyoming runs an approved state occupational safety and health plan, so ordinary farm safety goes to the state agency in Cheyenne9. But Wyoming is among the states that did not take on field sanitation or most agricultural labor camps, and those are enforced here by the federal Wage and Hour Division under the Secretary of Labor’s order9. Camps tied to egg, poultry or red meat production and to post-harvest processing stay with the state.

Until 30 September 2026 there was a regulation describing how these agencies coordinated farmworker enforcement. It was removed and reserved that day22. This page therefore names the agencies and numbers directly instead of pointing at a coordination rule a reader would now find empty.

On the Spanish version

The Spanish page is a peer, not a courtesy: the same answers, the same sources, the same column C. It is linked above the threshold rather than tucked in a footer, because a page whose readers include people who work in Spanish and read legal English with difficulty should not make the second language harder to find than the first.

The law supports the instinct. The farmworker act requires disclosure “in English or, as necessary and reasonable, in Spanish or another language common to migrant or seasonal agricultural workers who are not fluent or literate in English”11, and the H‑2A work contract must be in a language the worker understands1. A farm whose crew reads Spanish and whose hiring page is English-only has already fallen behind its own paperwork.

The translation is machine-assisted and reviewed, which is worth saying out loud: it has not been checked by a certified translator, and nothing on either page is legal advice.

Who keeps this page

The farm does not build web pages and should not have to. Behind this demonstration, the county’s chapters of the Future Farmers of America — Chief Washakie FFA at Worland High School, and Ten Sleep FFA — are the hoped-for keepers: bringing pages like this one to the farms, and each farm’s news back to its page. Whether they will be interested is not yet known.

The pilot program that would pay for the first dozen pages like this one is proposed at washakie‑ai.online.

About this page

It is built in the GYRO format: plain answers first, every source previewed in the middle, the detail last. It is a handful of plain files the farm owns outright — no monthly fee, no login for anyone who reads it, nothing to keep up but the words. It works on a phone in the pickup and prints properly for a kitchen table or a bulletin board.

Nothing here is legal advice. It is a plain reading of public law with every source shown, written so that a worker can take the citation to someone who can act on it. Where the law is unsettled, this page says that it is unsettled rather than choosing an answer.

What went wrong

The format asks every page to record at least one thing that went wrong. Here it is, and it is about this page’s own method.

  • The first draft of the pay answer carried a dollar figure. It was an adverse effect wage rate, carefully sourced, and wrong three times over: it predated the August 2026 rate notice, it was a single statewide number in a system that had stopped working that way in 2023, and the method that produced it had been held unlawful five weeks before this page was written. All three were caught in checking rather than in drafting. The answer now carries no number at all and sends the reader to where the rate actually lives — which is a better page than the one with the figure in it would have been, for every reader, in every future season.
  • A related near miss. A nearby farmworker services office was going to be listed with its address and telephone number. The listing turned out to rest on a page with a 2022 copyright and a federal funding table from 2019. Publishing an unverified telephone number for a worker in trouble is the worst failure this page could have, so the office is not listed until someone has called it.
