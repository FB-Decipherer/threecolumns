What has been happening

A plain account of how this site has changed and why, newest first. It is kept so that anyone returning after a few weeks — including the person who writes it — can pick the thread back up without reading a commit history. Each entry says what changed and what it was for; the machinery is in column C where it belongs.

Where it stands

This site is the technical reference for the Three Columns format, and it is written in the format it describes: plain answers on the left, every source previewed in the middle, technical detail on the right.

It currently carries twenty-three previewed sources, a search box that indexes the page from its own contents, and three sub-pages — D, the setup sheet a subject never has to see; E, standing up a domain; and F, what owning one of these sites actually consists of.

Two sample sites live under it, at /sites/: an invented farm and an invented tree service, both marked SAMPLE, both built exactly as a real one would be.

Every page of every site now has a copy elsewhere. All 32 pages across the four live sites are sent to the Internet Archive by a command on the author’s machine, dated, and each capture is verified to exist rather than assumed from the reply. What that is worth, and the three things it is not, are answered in column A.

The argument has not changed since the first day. One topic, one HTML file, no server, no database, no subscription, no vendor — and every claim on the page backed by a source the reader can check without leaving the column.

What has been happening

25 September 2026 — the property underneath the others, finally named

It came out of a question Walter asked about something else entirely: does the link checker handle internal links, or only external ones? Answering it turned up that internal links are checked by nothing at all — and then a second question followed from it. What about ibid., the footnote convention: is it still needed here?

It is not, and the reason is worth more than the answer. Every reference on these pages names what it points at, never where it sits. A numeral means the same thing however the reader arrived — from the top, from a numeral in the third column, from a search, from somebody else’s link into the middle of the page. Ibid. means “the note immediately before”, and on a page read that way there is no before.

Walter’s own summing up was “yet another advantage to this, logical simplicity”, and that is right, with one refinement now written into column A: the simplicity is the consequence, not the aim. What is actually being refused is relative addressing. Four things follow from that refusal, and until today all four were being enjoyed without anybody naming the cause:

  • Nothing needs abbreviating, because a source exists once as one card and citing it three times costs three numerals.
  • A machine can check the references, because a numeral has a target that either resolves or does not. “See above” has no target, so nothing can test it.
  • An archived copy resolves on its own, since nothing in it depends on a reader’s route through it.
  • A quoted paragraph still refers to something after it has been cut out.

The Chicago Manual of Style reached the same conclusion for print at its seventeenth edition, discouraging ibid. on the ground that it “risks confusing the reader”. Its citation chapters had to add permalinks and electronic page numbers in the same revision, which is a fair indication of what forced the change. That is source 23.

This is recorded as a principle rather than a preference because it is the thing to protect first if the format is ever altered. A change that makes any reference depend on position would cost all four benefits at once, and would do it silently.

25 September 2026 — a copy of every page, kept by someone who is not the author

The format’s whole proposition is do not take my word for it, check. Until today that sentence had one hole in it: everything a reader could check was served from an address the author pays for, on a host whose terms the author has to accept, and nothing outside that arrangement recorded what any page had ever said. Walter asked whether there was an off-the-shelf way to fix it. There is.

A new command, ARCHIVE SITES, finds every site on the volume the way the link checker does — a folder with both a CNAME and an index.html — turns each page into the address it is served at, and submits it to the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine. Thirty-two pages across four sites, and new sites are picked up without anyone editing a list.

Three decisions are worth recording because each one could have gone the other way:

  • Session notes are excluded. The walk found three working documents under claude/ on washakie-ai.online and would happily have made them permanent. They are published only as a side effect of publishing everything else. Superseded drafts and anything awaiting deletion are left out for the same reason: a public, permanent record of withdrawn work is not a service to anybody.
  • Success is measured by outcome. Submitting a page returns a reply saying the request was accepted, which is not the same as a capture existing. The command waits, then asks the archive page by page whether today’s capture is really there, and reports only what it can confirm. Anything it cannot confirm is named rather than glossed.
  • It asks first. A capture is public and effectively permanent; removing one means writing to the Internet Archive, not deleting a file. So the command prints exactly what it is about to send and waits for a yes.

The answer in column A sets out what this buys: a date the author did not set, proof against quiet editing, a citation that still resolves when the domain does not, and — with a free account — the cited sources captured alongside the page that cites them. It is also honest about what it is not. Not a backup: the archive keeps the page as a reader sees it, not the files it was built from. Nothing for search ranking. No visitors.

The reason it earns a place in the format rather than being a convenience: an archived copy is the one link in the chain the author does not control.

24 September 2026 — the word that was never on the page

Walter, reading all of this back: “I don’t remember if Transparency is one of the many advantages offered by this project, but surely that belongs somewhere.” It was not. The word appeared nowhere on any site in the family — the only match anywhere was a line of stylesheet setting a shape to transparent.

That is worse than an omission, because transparency is not one advantage among several here. It is the property every other rule is an expression of: sources previewed in place, a failure recorded in every column C, a defect published rather than fixed quietly, an argument dropped for being uncheckable, checks named rather than merely run, dead links marked rather than deleted, samples that say they are samples, and this page. Eight rules, one property, never named.

It is named now, in column A, and framed the way it has to be framed to mean anything: every one of those rules costs the author something. That cost is the whole of their value.

24 September 2026 — this page, and what it replaced

Until today the record of what had changed here was two one-line cards at the top of the middle column, and a private working log that is addressed to a machine, runs to 71,000 characters, and is wrong about its own status the moment anything is published. Neither is a record a person can return to.

This page is the third internal link, and it sits last in that block: the two most recent changes first, then the whole story. It was very nearly called a Synopsis; Chronicle is the better word, because a synopsis is a condensation and what this is, is a dated record. The block at the top is the synopsis; everything under it is the chronicle.

24 September 2026 — ownership got a straight answer, and the answer found a fault

Every page in this family had been saying a subject owns their site outright. That sentence appeared once, with nothing behind it. Page F now takes it apart: you own the domain and the words, you do not own the hosting, and the reason that is survivable is that everything you own is portable in an afternoon.

Writing it turned up a real defect. Every repository here carries an MIT license at its root — right for the format, which is meant to be free — but the file sits above sites/ as well, so the grant reaches a subject's own words and prices. Harmless for two invented samples; a genuine mistake for a real business at handover. It is recorded on Page F as a live defect and has not been fixed, because the format asks that failures be published rather than tidied away.

24 September 2026 — the most predictable objection, finally answered

“Why not just keep my Facebook page?” is the first thing anyone says about all of this, and until today the reference did not mention Facebook once. The answer now rests on three things a reader can check rather than on the usual folklore about link reach, which is unsourceable and is explicitly declined on the page: a feed is ordered by recency so an answer sinks whether or not it is still true; automated clients are asked not to fetch the pages at all, so nothing can archive, verify or cite them; and the address belongs to somebody else. The recommendation is both, never migration.

24 September 2026 — a second sample, and the samples became reachable

The farm sample showed the format carrying a place. The new tree service sample shows it carrying a problem: a stranger standing in their own yard cannot tell whether a contractor is insured, and the usual proof is a folded certificate in a glovebox. So its first answer is the insurance, and every line of it links to a check somebody else keeps.

Separately and more embarrassingly: both samples have lived at this domain for days while nothing here linked to them. Only the grant proposal did. Column A now lists them.

23 September 2026 — the page became its own index

Column A's answers are folded shut, and a browser's own find bar does not reliably look inside a folded answer — so a reader could be told a word was absent when the page plainly contained it. A search box now builds its index from the page's own contents at load, opens the right column, unfolds the answer and marks it.

The same day, the Internal links block went from one recent change to two — one was too thin to be a record — and the passage about sources arriving at the reader was split in two, so that the Back button carries equal weight with the card that comes to you. Both ends of the interruption, not one.

22 September 2026 — a team of one, and a word doing two jobs

The strongest claim this format makes is about cost, and it turns on communication rather than typing: one person means zero lines of communication, and the cost of more climbs like a hockey stick. The page now argues it with a drawing — the connectors across the vertices of regular polygons — and pairs it with a list of the industry job titles the arrangement simply removes.

Also: “publish” had been doing two jobs, meaning both stand a site up and send a change live, which made the GitHub answer needlessly frightening. Split apart, with new answers on changing the words later and on who sets a site up in the first place.

21 September 2026 — the reference caught up with the sites

For a while this page described less than the sites built from it actually did, which is the wrong way round for a reference. The format's own features were surveyed from the live sites and written in: the quality-assurance checks, named on the page rather than merely run; Phone view shown to every reader here, because on this site the layout is the subject; and the sites/ folder, so samples live at this domain rather than costing a new one each.

17–19 September 2026 — the foundations

Phone view built as a feature of the format and fitted to every site at once; a whole column made clickable as its own button; reference numerals set on white discs so they read as controls. A share card found to be serving the wrong site's image and restored, then replaced by one generated from three fields instead of drawn by hand — the first thing here to stop being a manual job.

Still open

  • The sources are not yet archived, only the pages. Capturing what a page links to needs a free archive.org account and its two keys in a file beside the command. Without them the pages are kept and the sources they cite are not — which is backwards for a format whose middle column exists so a reader can go and look. Five minutes of setup, not yet done.
  • The license defect above. Not fixed. It needs the format permissively licensed and a subject's content reserved, which one file at a repository root cannot do.
  • The link checker reports a link dead that is not. One source card points at facebook.com, which by design refuses automated clients. The site's own notes name it as a known exception, but the checking command does not yet understand the difference.
  • How a family of sites should be addressed — one domain with folders, one domain with subdomains, or a domain each — is undecided, and the answer changes what twelve sites cost.
  • This page exists only here. The other four sites have the same history and no chronicle yet.
  • It does not go all the way back. The record begins on 17 September; the site’s first commit was 10 September. Eleven commits from that first week are not described here, and their commit messages — vv, wer, 3 — will not help anybody reconstruct them.
